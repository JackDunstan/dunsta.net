A Special Investigation  ·  February 1993 — Thirty Years On
Investigative Feature

Welcome to the Wasteland

How the world's biggest rock band, a treeless speedway on the edge of Melbourne, and a culture of systematic contempt for audiences combined to produce the worst concert in Australian history — and why it finally forced the industry to change.

Rock Archaeology Editorial
Calder Park Raceway  ·  1 February 1993

The Victorian Ombudsman's office does not, as a rule, concern itself with rock concerts. Its mandate runs to the conduct of public officials, bureaucratic grievances, the machinery of government. But in the winter of 1993, the complaints began arriving — and kept arriving — until they numbered enough to fill a hundred-page formal report. The subject of that report was a single afternoon at a motor racing circuit in Melbourne's outer north-west, and a chain of decisions so comprehensively negligent that they left two thousand people hospitalised, tens of thousands stranded in the dark along a freeway, and the live music industry in Victoria permanently changed. The band, Guns N' Roses, played beautifully. Almost everything else was a disaster.

I

The Biggest Band in the World

To understand the scale of what was attempted at Calder Park, you first need to understand the scale of Guns N' Roses in early 1993. Formed in Los Angeles in 1985 from veterans of the city's hard rock scene, the band's name was itself a composite — part LA Guns, part Hollywood Rose. Their 1987 debut, Appetite for Destruction, was a phenomenon: it reached number one on the Billboard charts, spawned a run of enduring singles, and remains to this day the highest-selling debut album in US chart history, with 30 million copies sold worldwide. By any measure, these were not merely a popular band. They were a generational event.

The follow-up, released in 1991 as the twin volumes Use Your Illusion I and II, was a more sprawling, ambitious, and contested statement — both records released simultaneously, both entering the Billboard Top 10 in the same week. The tour that followed was proportionally monstrous: 194 shows across 31 countries over more than two years, one of the longest and most logistically complex tours in live music history. The band had outgrown stadiums. They were booking racetracks.

Australia was a brief detour on the tail end of this juggernaut: two shows in a week before New Zealand, with Michael Chugg — the country's most powerful concert promoter — at the helm. The first, at Eastern Creek Raceway outside Sydney on January 30, drew 80,000 people and went without serious incident. A 2011 radio poll would later vote it the greatest rock show ever staged in Australia. The stage, in every sense, seemed set.

II

The Venue: Thirty Kilometres from Anywhere

Calder Park Raceway sits on a flat, arid, treeless plain thirty kilometres north-west of the Melbourne CBD. It began life as a dirt motorsport circuit in the early 1960s, and was transformed in the 1970s when professional racing driver Bob Jane — later the founder of a national tyre chain — purchased the property and, inspired by a visit to NASCAR in the United States, dramatically expanded it. By the early 1980s, Calder Park was one of the country's premier motorsport venues.

It was also, as a concert venue, a fundamentally hostile environment. There was no shade. There was no natural shelter. It was not serviced by public transport — attendees had to drive and park in an improvised dirt carpark adjacent to the track, or take a special shuttle bus from the city. There was no infrastructure designed for the welfare of large, stationary human crowds in the heat of a Melbourne summer. It was, in short, a place designed for cars.

None of this deterred the promoters. Seventy-five thousand tickets were sold. The show would be the last concert ever held at Calder Park.

III

The Day Before: Gouging Begins Early

The disaster did not wait for the day itself to begin. Dedicated fans started arriving at the racetrack the night before — the evening of January 31 — camping out to secure the best vantage points. They brought their own food and water. They were, in this, more organised than the people who had sold them tickets.

The merchandise vendors, noting a captive and captive-only audience, were already working the queues by nightfall. The official tour t-shirt was being hawked for up to $75 — a considerable sum in 1993. The early-arriving fans, having spent their disposable cash on souvenirs while queuing in the evening heat, would find themselves stripped of their own provisions at the gate the following morning, with no money left to replace them at the prices they were about to encounter inside.

IV

The Confiscation Policy

The policy that awaited concert-goers at the entrance to Calder Park on the morning of February 1 was not hidden. It had been stipulated by the organisers. What it prohibited was, in retrospect, almost an act of aggression against a crowd about to spend a summer day on an exposed, shadeless plain.

The banned items list included alcohol — routine at such events — but also food, drinks of any kind, eskies, umbrellas, cameras, and sunscreen. Burly security teams enforced the policy at the gates with thoroughness. Bottles of water were confiscated. Families who had packed lunches handed them over. People who had brought sunscreen — basic self-protection in a February Melbourne heatwave — surrendered it, and were directed instead to a single communal sunscreen station: one booth, one drum, one enormous queue.

Inside the venue, those queues continued. Having waited up to five hours just to enter the grounds, fans then faced waits of ninety minutes or more to purchase food or water from the limited vendors inside. The price of a small bottle of water had reached five dollars. Later, as conditions worsened, it would climb to seven — a figure that would later attract specific mention in the Ombudsman's report, with a Mr Whippy van among the vendors cited for price gouging. In real terms, for 1993, these were extraordinary sums.

"People who tried to scrounge water from the bathroom facilities had their drink bottles taken from them by zealous security guards. Arguments and scuffles broke out."

Eyewitness account, compiled in the Victorian Ombudsman's report, 1993

The bathroom facilities, meanwhile, had descended into squalor by mid-morning. The permanent amenities were overwhelmed. Improvised enclosures — cloth screens around patches of open ground — were pressed into service as emergency toilets. The Ombudsman's report would later describe the resulting area as a saturated and malodorous swamp. It was 10 o'clock in the morning.

V

The Heat, the Storm, and the Reckoning

February 1, 1993 was a sweltering Melbourne summer's day. Temperatures reached into the high thirties by mid-morning and continued to climb. Calder Park Raceway offered no natural shade whatsoever — no trees, no covered areas, no structures to shelter beneath. The 75,000 people packed onto the infield had no protection from the sun except what they had brought with them, and what they had brought had been taken away at the gate.

Nearly two thousand people were treated for sunstroke before the headliners even took the stage. A number were hospitalised. One attendee, recalling the experience years later, described being jammed like a sardine into Section A in what seemed like 40-degree heat. It was not hyperbole: the official temperature readings were consistent with those accounts.

Then, in the mid-afternoon, Melbourne performed one of its famous meteorological reversals. A violent wind rose. Thunder followed. Lightning split the sky. Within minutes, a torrential downpour drenched the crowd — and, in a darkly comic coda to the sunscreen confiscation, thousands of dehydrated fans simply opened their mouths to catch the only free water they would receive all day.

VI

The Band: Brilliant, Fractured, and Barely Speaking

While the crowd endured the elements, the internal state of Guns N' Roses was, if anything, more volatile. The band that arrived in Australia in early 1993 was not the band that had recorded Appetite for Destruction. Original drummer Steven Adler had been fired in 1990; rhythm guitarist Izzy Stradlin had quit in 1991. The replacements — Matt Sorum on drums, Gilby Clarke on rhythm guitar, Dizzy Reed on keyboards — were capable musicians, but the band's original chemistry had been fundamentally altered.

More critically, the relationship between Axl Rose and Slash had deteriorated to the point of open hostility, largely over Slash's decision to guest with Michael Jackson. Rose had publicly condemned Jackson following allegations of child abuse. When Slash flew to Paris to perform at a Jackson pay-per-view show regardless, the fracture became irreparable. The band's long-time manager, Doug Goldstein, later described his role on the tour as essentially that of a buffer, keeping Rose unaware of the depth of resentment building among his bandmates.

"I kept them, kind of, separate. Probably the best thing I ever did for that band was not let Axl know how the other band members couldn't stand him... He'd leave the stage and they'd have to stand on stage and continue to play."

Doug Goldstein, GNR manager — Nine Network's The Rundown, 2018

By Calder Park, the dysfunction had become logistical. Rose and the rest of the band travelled to the venue on separate helicopters. They stayed at different hotels. On the day of the show, Rose entered the stage from the opposite side to his bandmates. The world's biggest rock band arrived at Australia's most infamous concert in a state of active internal collapse.

Goldstein, reflecting on the day twenty-five years later, was candid about what the organisation knew going in: "It's important to me, crowd safety... We pretty much knew." The concert, he acknowledged, never really stood a chance of succeeding in any meaningful operational sense.

And yet, once they played, they played. Whatever was happening offstage, whatever fractures were spreading through the band's internal architecture, the music was the one thing that held. The opening notes of Welcome to the Jungle hit the sodden, sunburned crowd like a defibrillator. For the next two hours, the accumulated miseries of the day were, for many, subsumed entirely.

The set drew generously from both Appetite and the Use Your Illusion records, weaving between the raw kinetic fury of the early material and the overblown stadium-rock grandeur of the newer songs. November Rain unfurled with piano and orchestration over a drenched racetrack under a darkening sky. Paradise City closed the show with 75,000 voices. Rock writer Rod Whitfield, who had attended both the 1989 Appetite shows and the Calder Park gig, later drew a telling distinction: the earlier show, he said, was the most rip-roaring, ballsy rock concert he had ever witnessed. Calder Park was something different — an overblown spectacle, an empire at its maximum extension, brilliant and excessive in equal measure.

Drummer Duff McKagan, asked about the Melbourne show in a 2012 interview, summoned a single image: "a fucking sea of people." And nothing else. The band's official website, in its detailed tour chronology, reduced the entire event to two sentences: the Super Bowl was watched on television; the show went ahead despite torrential rain and broke local attendance records.

VIII

The Aftermath: Stranded in the Dark

When the music stopped and the band were helicoptered away, 75,000 people were left to navigate their way home from a remote raceway with no functioning transport infrastructure, in the dark, after a storm.

The dirt carpark had been transformed by the downpour into a bog. Thousands of cars were trapped, unable to move. Those that could escape quickly found themselves in a single-track exit that was catastrophically beyond capacity. The lighting was inadequate. People sat in gridlocked vehicles for hours. The air smelled of mud and something worse.

During the show

The shuttle bus service — the only public transport option — ran its final departures the moment GNR left the stage. Thousands who had planned to use the buses to return to the city arrived at the bus stop to find it empty.

Post-show

With both the bus service terminated and the carpark immobilised in mud, fans began walking. They walked out to the Calder Highway. Many tried to hitchhike. Others simply kept walking — along the Calder Freeway and the Tullamarine Freeway — towards Melbourne.

Late night

Radio MC Lee Simon, who had been enlisted as the day's MC at the last minute, would recall the scene years later with visible distress: a stream of people walking along the freeway, in the dark, after midnight. "It was like a scene out of The Living Dead," he told Nine Network's The Rundown in 2018. "It was just a dreadful way to endure a show."

The weeks that followed

Complaints began arriving at the Victorian Ombudsman's office. They arrived in sufficient quantity, and with sufficient specificity, that the office opened a formal investigation.

IX

The Ombudsman's Report and Its Legacy

The Victorian Ombudsman's subsequent report ran to one hundred pages. It detailed, with bureaucratic precision, what had gone wrong: the inadequate sanitary facilities; the confiscation of water and sunscreen from attendees facing a forecast heatwave; the price-gouging by vendors who held a captive market; the failure to provide shade or shelter on an exposed plain in a southern Australian summer; the collapse of the transport arrangements once the event ended. It was a systematic accounting of a systematic failure.

Its conclusions were not merely historical. Several of the report's recommendations were adopted as law, and form part of the regulatory architecture governing live events in Victoria to this day. The most consequential of these: the requirement for concert organisers to provide free drinking water at all outdoor music events, and to supply adequate shade and shelter. These obligations, now routine and unremarkable — the kind of thing attendees at contemporary festivals take entirely for granted — were, in significant part, born from what happened at Calder Park on February 1, 1993.

What Changed: The Ombudsman's Legacy in Victorian Law

  • Free drinking water must be provided at all outdoor concerts in Victoria — a direct result of the Calder Park investigation.
  • Adequate shade and shelter became a legal requirement at outdoor music events in the state.
  • Transport planning obligations for remote-venue concerts were substantially strengthened.
  • The report laid the groundwork for the broader regulatory framework that governs major outdoor events in Victoria today.
  • Calder Park Raceway itself never hosted another concert. The GNR show was the last.
X

The Band: An Empire in Its Last Days

The Calder Park show sits, in retrospect, at a peculiar inflection point in the history of Guns N' Roses. The Use Your Illusion tour continued for another several months before limping to its close. The band did not release another album of original material under that name for fifteen years. Slash would leave in 1996, citing irreconcilable differences with Rose. The "classic" lineup — the one that had built the mythology — was effectively finished.

What Calder Park captured, then, was something specific: the band at maximum scale, maximum internal dysfunction, and maximum cultural reach, simultaneously. The circus had reached its most extravagant performance. The tent poles were already cracking.

The contrast with the 1989 Appetite shows is instructive. Rod Whitfield's description of that earlier incarnation — hungry, raw, dangerous, alive — stands in direct opposition to the Calder Park experience. The 1993 band entered the venue on separate helicopters and exited on the same. The Scarface theme — Tony Montana's story of a man isolated by his own megalomania, surrounded by enemies, burning through everything he built — played over the PA as they walked on. It is possible nobody noticed. It is possible everybody did.

XI

What People Remember

Here is the uncomfortable truth that the Ombudsman's report, for all its rigour, cannot quite contain: most of the people who were there remember it, at some level, with something approaching pride. Not pride in the organisation — contempt for that remains universal. But pride in having endured it, in being part of something so singularly excessive, in standing in a swamp in a hailstorm and watching the biggest band on earth play Paradise City while 75,000 people screamed back every word.

Eyewitness accounts collected online consistently make the same observation that the Museum of Lost Things noted in its retrospective: not a single person complained about the music. Attendees who describe fainting from the heat, being stranded on freeways at midnight, watching their cars sink into mud — all of them note, in the same breath, that the show itself was extraordinary. One attendee, recalling how she fainted on purpose to get emergency water and then walked herself into the Section A front area within metres of Axl Rose, described it as the best concert she ever attended. She was seventeen.

This is not a contradiction. It is, rather, the definition of a certain kind of live music experience — one in which the event exceeds the performance, the chaos becomes part of the memory, and the music is both the reason you endured everything and the thing that makes the endurance feel, in hindsight, worth it. The industry should not have permitted it. The organisation was inexcusable. The Victorian Ombudsman was right.

And 75,000 people still talk about it.

The Guns N' Roses concert at Calder Park Raceway, Melbourne, took place on 1 February 1993 as part of the Use Your Illusion world tour. It remains the last concert ever staged at the venue. The Victorian Ombudsman's report into the event contributed directly to the regulatory framework governing outdoor concerts in Victoria. The band's classic lineup reunited in 2016 for the Not in This Lifetime tour.
